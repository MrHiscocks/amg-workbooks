Build a convincing case for your project.

A short, practical guide to explaining the problem, setting broad aims, recognising limitations and using feedback well.

Support, not extra work. Read the guidance here, then write the assessed section in the separate report template.
01 · Purpose & overview

What do you need to do?

Explain why your proposed project is worthwhile and realistic, then show how feedback helped you understand it more clearly.

Your Discussion has four jobs

1
Establish the problemIntroduce the organisation and explain the current problem, who it affects, its impact and why it has enough scope for a substantial project.
2
Describe the broad aimsState the main outcomes the proposed solution should achieve. Keep them connected to real user and organisational needs.
3
Explain possible limitationsIdentify realistic constraints and explain what each could mean for the project or final system.
4
Present, gather feedback and refineDiscuss the proposal with an appropriate third party, consider their comments and show what changed or became clearer.
Report sectionYour clear written account.
PresentationYour proposal explained to a relevant person.
Feedback trailComments, decisions and refinement.
Keep it problem-first

This section is not the detailed design. Explain what needs solving and why before committing to features.

02 · What good looks like

Build towards Band 3

Better work is not simply longer. It is more specific, better connected, technically accurate and visibly refined after feedback.

The three-band ladder

1–2 marksBand 1

Limited scope; more than one broad aim described; at least one limitation; feedback used only a little.

3 marksBand 2

Suitable problem; realistic aims; main limitations clearly described; feedback improves understanding.

Target quality
4–5 marksBand 3

Substantial problem; fully described aims; detailed limitations; feedback fully considered and used where appropriate.

Substantial problemEnough genuine depth and scope for the full project.
Fully explained aimsClear outcomes, reasons and appropriate technical vocabulary.
Detailed limitationsReal constraints with effects—not token disadvantages.
Visible refinementFeedback decisions clearly improve or confirm understanding.
The strongest thread: problem → broad aim → limitation → feedback decision. The reader should never have to guess how one part connects to the next.
03 · Section toolkit

Four tools. One clear case.

Select a workshop tool when you need help shaping that part of your report.

Diagnose the problem

Move from what currently happens to a specific, evidenced need. Do not jump straight to “they need an app”.

Current process
→
Cause
→
Impact
→
Evidence
1Who experiences the problem, and when?
2What errors, delays, duplication or missed decisions result?
3What evidence shows this is real and substantial?
Useful frame: “Currently, [role] uses [process]. Because [cause], [specific problem] occurs, resulting in [impact].”

Write outcome-led aims

A broad aim says what meaningful improvement is needed and for whom. The detailed interface belongs later.

Outcome verb
+
User or process
+
Needed improvement
+
Purpose
1Which problem or user need does this aim answer?
2What should improve—not merely what control will be built?
3How will this help the organisation or user?
Useful frame: “Enable [user/role] to [meaningful outcome] so that [problem impact is reduced].”

Explain realistic limitations

Name the real constraint, then explain what it could prevent or restrict. A label without an effect is not enough.

Constraint
→
Likely effect
→
Boundary or response
1Is the limitation specific to data, access, time, platform, scale or deployment?
2What does it mean for this project or its users?
3What realistic boundary or response follows?
Useful frame: “Because [constraint], the project may [effect]. Therefore, this version will [realistic boundary].”

Show the feedback decision

Receiving feedback is only the start. Make the decision and its effect on your understanding visible.

Comment
→
Accept, adapt or reject
→
Reason
→
Refinement
1Why was this person appropriate to give feedback?
2Which assumption, aim or limitation did the comment challenge?
3What changed—or what was confirmed—and why?
Useful frame: “[Stakeholder] suggested [point]. I [accepted/adapted/rejected] this because [reason], which [changed/confirmed] [specific understanding].”
04 · Common weaknesses

Watch for these

Most weaker Discussion sections do not fail because they are short. They fail because the reasoning is vague, disconnected or unrealistic.

Five common problems—and the repair

1
Too much backgroundA long history of the organisation hides the actual problem.
Repair itKeep only the context needed to understand the current process, users and impact.
2
Solution before problemThe report announces an app, database or dashboard before proving the need.
Repair itExplain the current workflow, cause and impact first. Let features answer evidence later.
3
Aims become a feature list“Add a login”, “add search”, “add buttons”.
Repair itRewrite each as a user or organisational outcome with a clear purpose.
4
Limitations are merely named“Time”, “security” or “hardware” appears with no consequence.
Repair itExplain exactly what the constraint may restrict and the boundary it creates.
5
Feedback is dumped, not usedComments are listed with no decision or visible refinement.
Repair itRecord the decision, justify it and show the before-and-after understanding.
Do not overclaimKeep scale, access, security and deployment promises realistic for the tools and evidence you actually have.
05 · Top tips

Keep it sharp

Six habits that make the section stronger without making it longer.

Advice from the workbench

01
Start with what happens now.A clear current process makes the problem credible.
02
Keep one logic thread.Every aim should answer a need; every limitation should affect the proposal.
03
Use technical vocabulary for precision.Accurate terms strengthen meaning; unnecessary jargon does not.
04
Explain consequences.After every problem or limitation, ask: “So what does this mean?”
05
Ask targeted feedback questions.Challenge scope, priorities, assumptions and missing needs—not simply “Do you like it?”
06
Cut repetition.If a sentence adds no new evidence, reason or connection, remove it.
Could a competent third party understand the problem, aims, limitations and feedback decisions without asking me to fill in the gaps?
